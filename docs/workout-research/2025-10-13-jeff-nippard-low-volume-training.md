## What the video contains

Nippard describes a 100-day personal experiment that reduced his training volume to mostly one or two hard working sets per exercise, averaging about 6.5 weekly sets per muscle. His split was upper, lower, rest, upper, lower, arms and delts, rest. This is a personal case study and programming example, not evidence that every Trak user should copy the routine.

## Approved Trak decisions

- Keep the current Weekly Muscle Score thresholds. The video does not justify changing the approved 12-set weekly target or the recovery checks at 16 and 20 sets.
- When a user has 30 minutes or less, enable a time-efficient strength prescription:
  - two working sets for a selected foundation movement;
  - one working set for a non-foundation accessory;
  - scale the exercise time estimate to the prescribed set count so the complete recommendation fits the stated time budget;
  - do not activate this merely because the goal is fat loss.
- Explicit specialization blocks retain their own capped set prescription instead of being reduced by the short-session rule.
- Show an effort reminder with safety context:
  - beginners keep 1–2 clean repetitions in reserve;
  - experienced users may take the final set of a selected stable-machine or isolation exercise to technical failure, meaning another clean repetition is not possible;
  - loaded compound and free-weight movements stop with 1–2 clean repetitions in reserve and are not prescribed to failure.
- No exercises were added from this video because its named movements already exist in Trak or are adequately represented by existing patterns.
- Recommendations still earn no points. Only completed strength sets update Weekly Muscle Score, and only completed activity updates Workout Time.

## Evidence check

- Multiple-set training generally produces greater hypertrophy than a single set, although the dose-response has diminishing returns: [Krieger 2010](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20300012/) and [Pelland et al. 2026](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41343037/).
- A reduced dose preserved prior adaptations in younger adults in one study, but older adults did not preserve muscle size equally well: [Bickel et al. 2011](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21131862/). This supports a low-volume option, not a universal optimum.
- In trained adults performing one set of nine exercises twice weekly for eight weeks, both momentary failure and stopping at two repetitions in reserve improved outcomes; failure modestly favored some hypertrophy measures but not strength or endurance: [Hermann et al. 2025](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40249908/).
- Meta-analytic evidence suggests that training closer to failure can favor hypertrophy, but does not establish that every set must reach momentary failure: [Robinson et al. 2024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38970765/) and [Refalo et al. 2023](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36334240/).
- Evidence is insufficient to treat lower resistance-training volume as inherently superior during caloric restriction: [Murphy and Koehler 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35146569/). A trial in trained men found both moderate- and higher-volume approaches could preserve lean mass during restriction: [Roth et al. 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36114738/).

## Not adopted

- Universal all-out failure on every exercise or set.
- Failure under a squat, hinge, free-weight press, or other setup where technique breakdown or being trapped under load raises risk.
- The claim that low volume is automatically the best approach during a calorie deficit.
- Personal strength records, body-composition scans, or the creator's results as proof of a general causal effect.
- Extremely high-volume examples or the creator's paid routine as Trak defaults.
